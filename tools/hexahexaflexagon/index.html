What is this thing?

A hexahexaflexagon is a flat paper hexagon that hides six different faces, discovered in 1939 by Arthur H. Stone and studied by the Princeton “Flexagon Committee” (Stone, Bryant Tuckerman, Richard Feynman and John Tukey). Martin Gardner’s December 1956 Scientific American column about it launched his famous Mathematical Games series. The first “hexa” is the number of faces, the second is the number of sides of the polygon.

The template

Mark a straight strip of 19 equilateral triangles like this (the animation uses the same colours):

The back is what you see when you flip the strip over left-to-right, so it reads right-to-left here. One triangle on each side stays blank — those two ends get glued to each other at the very end.

The folds

  1. Roll it up (9 folds): working along the strip, fold each pair of equal numbers on the back onto each other — 6 onto 6, 5 onto 5, 4 onto 4. Every 4, 5 and 6 disappears inside, and you are left with a shorter straight strip that looks exactly like the template of the simpler trihexaflexagon.
  2. Wrap the hexagon (3 folds): fold the rolled strip back on itself twice, so it wraps around into a hexagon, then fold the last little flap over.
  3. Glue: the two blank triangles now touch. Glue them and the loop is closed — one face shows all 1s, the other all 2s, and faces 3–6 wait inside.

Flexing, and the Tuckerman traverse

To flex: pinch a pair of adjacent triangles together (mountain fold), push the opposite corner inward, then open the hexagon from the middle. It blooms open showing a face that was hidden. Not every corner will open — if one refuses, try the next corner over.

Bryant Tuckerman found the fastest way to see every face: keep flexing at the same corner until it jams, then move one corner over. This Tuckerman traverse cycles through twelve flexes:

Faces 1, 2 and 3 appear three times per cycle; faces 4, 5 and 6 only once — the “rare” faces are genuinely three times harder to find.
